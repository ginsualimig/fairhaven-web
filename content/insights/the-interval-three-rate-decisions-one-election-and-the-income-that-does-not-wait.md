On 2 October the US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payrolls rose by **29,000** in September, against a Dow Jones consensus of **84,000**. July and August were revised down by a combined **60,000** — August from +162,000 to +133,000, July from a gain to a loss of 10,000. Unemployment edged up to **4.2%**. Five days later the minutes of the Federal Reserve’s 15–16 September meeting showed that most participants still assessed another increase in the federal funds rate “would likely be appropriate by year end.” In Singapore, CBRE reported on 30 September that Core CBD Grade A office rents rose **2.0%** in the third quarter to S$12.75 per square foot a month, the fastest quarterly gain since 2022, with vacancy down to **2.6%**. And on 14 October the Monetary Authority of Singapore left its policy settings unchanged, although all 10 analysts in a Reuters poll published on 9 October had expected it to tighten again.

None of those prints moves a policy setting on its own. Together they define the interval wholesale investors are now standing in: MAS has held, the FOMC decides on 27–28 October, the RBNZ on 28 October, and New Zealand votes on 7 November. One policy decision in, two to come and an election on 7 November is exactly the setting in which waiting feels like prudence. For New Zealand income and Singapore freehold, it is not a reason to wait.

In September we wrote that the RBNZ’s 25 basis point hike to 2.75% matched the street, and that the Statement’s own track — a December-quarter average of 2.81% — read as a pause in October and a move to 3% in December. That reading has not been retired. What October adds is a wider street split in New Zealand, a softer US labour print set against a still-hawkish Fed, and a change of second market. From this edition the desk covers New Zealand and Singapore.

This edition covers:

- The interval: what has printed since the 2 September hike, and why the NZ street has split four ways ahead of 28 October
- The US signal that did not arrive: a weak September payrolls print against Fed minutes that still lean towards one more hike this year
- Singapore: tightening through the exchange rate, not an interest rate, and why MAS held its settings on 14 October
- Why NZ income and Singapore freehold are different trades, and how wide the yield gap between them actually is
- New Zealand and Singapore market updates, including the foreign-buyer and stamp-duty rules on Singapore commercial property
- Four new listings on the market this month, each large enough to need a syndicate, with their deadlines

## 1. The Interval: Between the Hike and the Next Decision

The RBNZ’s Monetary Policy Committee lifted the OCR to **2.75%** on 2 September. The next decision is the Monetary Policy Review at 2pm on **28 October**; the December Monetary Policy Statement follows on **9 December**. Nothing between those dates is pre-committed. The Committee’s own words in September were that the future OCR path “is not pre-determined” and that its response to data “is not mechanical.”

The data since then has pulled in two directions. Stats NZ reported on 17 September that GDP rose **0.2%** in the June quarter, after **0.9%** in March, taking annual-average growth to **1.7%** (2.6% on the June quarter a year earlier). Construction was the largest contributor, up **2.7%** in the quarter — its largest rise since June 2023. According to NZ Adviser’s summary of the bank notes, all five major bank economics teams described the result as stronger than expected given the fuel shock running through the quarter. It was still a soft quarter. It was not a contraction.

On 6 October the NZIER Quarterly Survey of Business Opinion showed a net **40%** of firms expecting better general economic conditions, up from **14%** in the June quarter. Firms’ own trading activity told a different story: a net **1%** reported a decline in the September quarter. Cost pressure eased — a net **47%** of firms reported higher costs, down from **54%** — and NZIER read hiring difficulty as a sign that “spare capacity in the New Zealand economy is dissipating.”

That gap is not noise. It is the same uneven recovery the RBNZ described in September — export-exposed and regional New Zealand supported by strong commodity prices, household-facing sectors under pressure, particularly in Auckland and Wellington — now visible in a single survey.

The street has read it four ways. Westpac’s 9 October forecast update keeps an October pause and a 25 basis point hike to **3%** in December, while lifting its December-quarter CPI forecast to **4.2%** on higher fuel prices and a weaker New Zealand dollar, and cutting its 2026 house price forecast from a small rise to a **1.4%** decline. On the banks’ own latest notes, ASB (5 October) expects hikes at both October and December, taking the OCR to **3.25%** by year-end; ANZ (21 September) expects an October move, a December pause, then hikes in February and March to a peak of **3.50%**; BNZ (17 September) expects 25 basis points at every meeting until the OCR reaches **3.75%**. Do not average them. The desk’s job is to name the range: on the published RBNZ track and Westpac’s house view, 28 October is a hold; on ASB, ANZ, and BNZ, it is a hike.

> The RBNZ has not told the market whether 28 October is a pause or a hike. It has told the market the direction. For assets that already carry their income in the lease, the direction matters more than the date.

## 2. The Signal That Did Not Come: Soft US Jobs, a Fed Still Leaning to Hike

Ordinarily, a payrolls miss of that size would reopen the easing conversation. It did not. On 2 October CNBC reported that market-implied odds of the Fed holding at its 27–28 October meeting jumped to **82.8%** on CME FedWatch — a hold, not a cut. Average hourly earnings rose **3.0%** over the year, the slowest annual pace since May 2021.

The Fed itself has not moved off its September posture. On 16 September the FOMC raised the target range by 25 basis points to **3.75–4.00%**, unanimously, and the median projection put the federal funds rate at **4.1%** at the end of both 2026 and 2027 — consistent with one more quarter-point increase and no cut next year. The minutes released on 7 October repeated the lean: most participants saw another increase as likely appropriate by year end, while stressing that each meeting would be approached “with an open mind.”

For NZ commercial property the transmission pipe is unchanged from August and September: long-end yields, the US dollar, and the ceiling on the 2026 rate headwind. For Singapore the pipe runs through the S$NEER and domestic interbank rates, with three-month compounded SORA at about 1.24% against a Fed target range of 3.75–4.00%. A softer US labour market lowers the odds of an October Fed move. It does not reverse the direction of travel. The signal the sidelines are waiting for — a turn to easing in the US — did not come in September’s data, and the Fed’s own projections do not have it arriving in 2027.

> Investors waiting for the US to turn before committing to NZ income or Singapore freehold are waiting for a signal the Fed’s own median does not forecast.

## 3. Singapore: Tightening Through the Exchange Rate

MAS does not set an interest rate. It sets a path for the Singapore dollar nominal effective exchange rate, the S$NEER, inside an undisclosed policy band, and adjusts the band’s slope, width and centre at four scheduled reviews a year. On 14 April it increased the slope slightly. On 27 July it increased it again, “very slightly”, by less than in April, with no change to the width or the centre. The July statement said MAS Core Inflation was “projected to step up from July and remain elevated but should moderate discernibly from around mid-2027.”

The data since has followed that script. MAS and MTI reported on 23 September that core inflation rose to **2.2%** in August from 2.0% in July — the highest since September 2024, according to CNA — with headline inflation at **2.3%**. On 14 October MAS trimmed its 2026 forecasts to 1.5% to 2.0% for core inflation and around 2.0% for headline, from 1.5% to 2.5% for both, and projects both at 1.5% to 2.5% in 2027. Growth is running well ahead of plan: on 11 August MTI put second-quarter GDP **5.9%** higher than a year earlier (since revised to 6.2%) and first-half growth at 6.1%, and lifted its 2026 forecast to **4.5% to 5.5%** from 2.0% to 4.0%, citing the acceleration in global AI-related capital expenditure.

The street expected a third move. All 10 analysts polled by Reuters, in a poll published on 9 October, expected MAS to tighten on 14 October; Barclays’ Brian Tan, who looked for another slight increase in the slope, had argued that growth was feeding into inflation more mildly than MAS expected. MAS did not move. It said it “will therefore maintain the rate of appreciation of the policy band”, with “no change to the width of the band and the level at which it is centred”, and that the current band “will allow the S$NEER to continue to strengthen and lean against the anticipated near-term rise in imported inflation.” The same morning, the advance estimate from the Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry (METI) put third-quarter GDP **5.4%** higher than a year earlier, moderating from 6.2% in the second quarter, and **1.1%** higher than the previous quarter on a seasonally adjusted basis. MAS expects the economy’s positive output gap to narrow through 2027; METI will announce its 2026 and 2027 growth forecasts in November.

For property, the more useful number is the one MAS does not set. Three-month compounded SORA, the benchmark for SORA-linked Singapore property loans, was **1.24%** on MAS’s 14 October publication; CBRE recorded 1.15% on 23 July, up from a trough of 1.02% in April. A tighter currency path has not made local money expensive. That matters for an asset class that yields about 3.25% to 3.80% (CBRE’s March range for prime offices, as reported by Singapore Business Review): a lower income line can still carry modest leverage when the local base rate, before bank margins, is less than half the RBNZ’s OCR.

> Singapore tightens by letting its currency rise, not by raising the cost of borrowing. For a freehold buyer that is a firmer store of value with cheap local debt. It is not an income story.

## 4. Translate into Commercial Property: The Cost of the Interval

Waiting does not feel like a decision. It feels like prudence. Three policy decisions and an election inside 25 days make the case for prudence sound stronger than it is.

The arithmetic has changed with the second market, and it should be stated plainly. CBRE’s Asia Pacific Cap Rate Survey, as reported by Singapore Business Review, put indicative cap rates for Singapore Grade A offices in core locations at **3.25% to 3.80%** in March, and multifamily and build-to-rent at 3.65% to 4.15%; CBRE expects the office range to compress further. JLL’s 8 September review has prime Auckland industrial trading at **5.00% to 5.50%** and Christchurch at **5.75% to 6.50%**. Midpoint to midpoint, that is a gap of roughly **170 basis points** against Auckland and **260 basis points** against Christchurch. On a large-format asset, that spread is significant annual income, and Singapore does not pay it. What Singapore offers in its place is freehold tenure, a currency MAS is steering higher, and some of the deepest institutional liquidity in the region: Cushman & Wakefield counts **S$44.3 billion** of investment sales in the first nine months of 2026, already above the previous full-year peak of S$36.8 billion set in 2017, as reported by Singapore Business Review (Knight Frank, on its own count as reported in the trade press, has S$42.4 billion).

In New Zealand, the income book is the same one we described in September: outskirt industrial in South Auckland, regional commercial yielding above 8%, social housing on government-aligned net leases, healthcare and essential services. A hike on 28 October makes leveraged entry less comfortable. A hold does not make it cheaper. Neither outcome changes the rent roll. Replacement costs are up since 2022 and construction pipelines are soft — NZIER’s architects’ measure points to reduced commercial and government construction work — so if conditions improve, yield-compression risk remains asymmetric to the upside.

In Singapore the case is not yield, and it should not be sold as one. It is capital preservation: freehold tenure, Core CBD Grade A vacancy at **2.6%** on CBRE’s count with no major office supply before 2028 beyond Newport Tower in 2027, and an industrial market that JTC shows still rising — rents up **0.5%** and prices up **0.6%** in the second quarter, with occupancy at 89.1%. Cushman & Wakefield’s own read of the quarter, as reported by Singapore Business Review, is that investors are increasingly focused on income-driven returns over cap rate compression. At 3.25% to 3.80%, the income keeps a Singapore asset carrying. It is not what the buyer is being paid for.

> High-yielding outskirt assets in supply-constrained corridors do not require rate cuts to perform. They require vacancy. The rate decision is a timing question; the income is already in the lease.

## 5. New Zealand Market Update

New Zealand is navigating a period of genuine difficulty, and we think it is more useful to address that directly than to frame it otherwise.

OCR **2.75%** after consecutive hikes on 8 July and 2 September. Next decisions: **28 October** (Review) and **9 December** (Statement). The RBNZ’s September track is consistent with a December move to 3%; on their latest notes, three of the four major banks (ASB, ANZ, BNZ) expect a hike in October, and Westpac reaffirmed a hold on 9 October. Headline CPI was **4.1%** in the June quarter on fuel; the RBNZ expects inflation back inside the 1–3% band by mid-2027 and at the 2% midpoint by late 2027. Stats NZ’s September-quarter CPI is due later in October, and Westpac expects **3.7%**. That print, not the street debate, is the most important data point for the 28 October decision.

GDP rose **0.2%** in the June quarter and **1.7%** in the year to June (annual average). Business confidence has recovered sharply; trading activity has not yet followed. Households remain the weak lane — Westpac now expects house prices to fall **1.4%** in 2026, and expects high fuel costs and a weak housing market to crimp consumer demand into Christmas. The general election on **7 November** adds a layer of uncertainty to household and business decisions in the weeks after the Review.

For commercial property the practical implication is unchanged from July, August, and September: **this is an income cycle, not a capital appreciation cycle**. The listings that work in this market are the ones that pay through a hike, a hold, and an election result alike. Certainty is a lagging indicator. The income is already in the lease.

## 6. Singapore Market Update / The Freehold Case

In New Zealand the case is income. In Singapore the case is capital preservation, at a lower income. Both are valid. The mistake is applying the same framework to both.

Offices first. URA’s second-quarter statistics, released on 24 July, showed Central Region office rentals up **0.8%** after a 0.2% fall in the first quarter, prices up **0.4%**, and island-wide office vacancy edging up to **11.0%** from 10.8% as new stock arrived. The prime end is tighter than that average suggests: CBRE’s third-quarter figures have Core CBD Grade A rents up **3.7%** so far this year, and CBRE expects full-year growth above **5%**, the strongest since 2022. URA’s third-quarter commercial statistics are due on 23 October.

Industrial has now recorded 23 consecutive quarters of rental growth on CBRE’s reading of the JTC series. JTC’s all-industrial rental index rose **0.5%** in the second quarter and its price index **0.6%**, and CBRE puts prime logistics occupancy at **95.7%**.

For an offshore syndicate, zoning decides which Singapore assets are investable at all, and both Singapore assets featured this month are on commercial zoning. CBRE’s launch release puts 80/82 South Bridge Road on land zoned “Commercial” with a plot ratio of 4.2 under URA’s Master Plan 2025, and ERA describes 42–46 Smith Street as zoned for commercial use; URA’s published Master Plan 2025 land-use layer shows both sites as Commercial. That matters because IRAS charges Additional Buyer’s Stamp Duty only on residential property, and for a whole building with land, IRAS classifies the property by its Master Plan zoning: land zoned “Commercial” counts as wholly non-residential. A commercial or industrial building pays ordinary Buyer’s Stamp Duty and no ABSD, whether the buyer is a Singapore citizen, a foreigner or a company, and the Singapore Land Authority confirms that a foreign person can buy commercial and industrial property, and shophouses for commercial use, without approval under the Residential Property Act. For a syndicate vehicle, that is the difference between a deal that works and one that does not.

One line of caution: shophouses zoned “Residential with commercial at first storey” are mostly residential property for stamp duty, and ABSD on the residential share is **60%** for foreigners and **65%** for entities, including the trustee of a collective investment scheme.
