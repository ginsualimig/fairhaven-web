On 28 October the Reserve Bank of New Zealand's Monetary Policy Committee voted **4–3** to hold the Official Cash Rate at **2.75%**, with three members preferring a 25 basis point increase to 3.0%. ASB had put the market-implied odds of a hike at around 95% before the announcement (as reported by NZ Adviser); BNZ's research team said it was "frankly flabbergasted". The same day the US Federal Reserve held its target range at **3.75–4.00%** by 10 votes to 2, with both dissenters, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, preferring an increase. On 4 November Stats NZ reported that New Zealand's unemployment rate rose to **5.7%**; Westpac noted it is the highest since September 2015. On 6 November the US Bureau of Labor Statistics reported that nonfarm payrolls rose by **165,000** in October, against a Dow Jones forecast of **59,000**. And on 7 November New Zealand voted, and woke up to a result that does not yet say who governs.

None of those prints is a rate rise. Together they define the pause wholesale investors are now standing in: the RBNZ has stopped, the Fed has stopped, and the labour data in the two countries point in opposite directions. In October we wrote that the four major banks all picked a hike to 3% on 28 October, and that for assets which already carry their income in the lease the direction mattered more than the date. The RBNZ did not hike, and we did not call it. The direction has not changed: the Committee said in the same breath that tighter policy "is likely to be required".

This edition covers:

- The pause: why the RBNZ held on 28 October, what the Committee said would change its mind, and where the banks now expect the OCR to peak
- The US: a Fed that held with two votes to hike, and a payrolls print that did not need the pause to be good news
- New Zealand's two open questions: an 11-year-high unemployment rate, and an election whose official count on 27 November will shape, but may not settle, who governs
- Singapore: no new MAS decision since 14 October, a rising local base rate, and what that means for freehold
- What a pause means for the income-versus-yield arithmetic across NZ industrial and Singapore commercial property
- Three new listings on the market this month: a Crown-leased industrial holding in Auckland and two Singapore commercial buildings, each large enough to need a syndicate

## 1. The Pause: Why the RBNZ Held, and What Would Change Its Mind

The Committee's media release gave the headline reasons. Annual CPI inflation was **3.7%** in the September quarter, down from **4.1%** in June and lower than expected; wage growth has been subdued and medium-term inflation expectations stable. But core inflation measures have risen to around the top of the 1 to 3 percent target band, and the Committee noted that 56% of the items in the CPI basket, on a weighted basis, rose by more than 3% in the year to September. The RBNZ now expects annual inflation to climb to **4.4%** in the December quarter, largely on current fuel futures prices, and notes that the trade-weighted New Zealand dollar has fallen by around 3% since the September Statement.

The split was not about direction. It was about timing and risk. Anna Breman, Carl Hansen, Prasanna Gai and Rebecca Williams favoured a hold: inflation had been lower than expected, the indirect effects of fuel prices smaller than feared, and they stressed the risk of a weak recovery in household consumption and investment. Paul Conway, Hayley Gourley and Karen Silk favoured an immediate 25 basis point rise to 3.0%, on the grounds that core inflation has been above target-consistent levels for some time and that acting now would reduce the risk of needing to do more later. The Committee's own summary was that members "agreed that tighter monetary policy is likely to be required but remains conditional on the economic outlook". The Record of Meeting adds that information arriving over the next six weeks "could meaningfully change the appropriate policy stance in either direction", including, if the world economy turned sharply down, towards easing.

The street's response was to keep the direction and soften the date. On their own post-decision notes, ANZ (28 October, reaffirmed 4 November) expects three 25 basis point increases, in December, February and March, taking the OCR to **3.50%**; BNZ (28 October) expects a rise on 9 December and a **3.75%** peak; Westpac (28 October) expects a December rise and a peak of **4.00%** in September 2027; Kiwibank (29 October) expects **3.00%** and then **3.25%** in 2027. ASB also expects December, February and March to **3.50%**, and NZIER just one more increase, in late 2027 (both as reported by NZ Adviser). Even the bulls hedged. Westpac wrote on 28 October that even the December rise it forecasts "should not be regarded as a done deal", and after the labour data its economists called it "far from a done deal"; Kiwibank's view is that a December rise is "more likely than not". NZ Adviser reported on 9 November, citing Westpac and BNZ, that market pricing implied a 60 to 65% chance of a rise on 9 December, and that the two-year swap rate had fallen by about 40 basis points from its level before the hold, to around 3.7%.

> The RBNZ has told the market the direction again. What it has not told the market is the date. For an asset that carries its income in the lease, the difference between a December rise and a February rise is a financing question, not an income question.

## 2. The US: A Fed on Hold With Two Votes to Hike

The Federal Open Market Committee's 28 October statement kept the target range at **3.75–4.00%**, where it has been since the quarter-point increase of 16 September. The text was mostly unchanged, with one notable upgrade: "Economic activity is expanding at a strong pace", replacing "solid". It also said that "Inflation remains elevated", and closed with "The Committee will deliver price stability." Two members, Lisa Cook and Beth Hammack, voted against the decision because they preferred to raise the range by a quarter of a percentage point. In his press conference Chairman Kevin Warsh said the third quarter appeared to have brought an even more rapid advance than the first half, led by healthy consumer spending, "likely running above 3 percent growth", and business investment "likely rising at about a double-digit pace".

The labour data on 6 November was consistent with that description. The BLS reported nonfarm payroll employment up **165,000** in October, against an average monthly gain of 48,000 over the prior 12 months and a Dow Jones forecast of **59,000**. The unemployment rate fell to **4.1%** from 4.2%, and average hourly earnings rose 0.4% on the month for a 3.1% annual increase. Earlier months were revised up: August to **186,000**, September from 29,000 to **60,000**. In October we wrote about a payrolls miss that did not reopen the easing conversation. November's print closes it further: the signal investors on the sidelines were waiting for, a US turn to easing, has not arrived, and two members of the Committee wanted to go the other way.

For New Zealand and Singapore commercial property the transmission pipe is the same as in every edition since August: long-end yields, the US dollar, and the ceiling on the global rate headwind. The RBNZ itself pointed to it on 28 October, noting that global long-term interest rates have increased and that some central banks have raised policy rates. The FOMC next meets on 8–9 December; its statement lands a day after the RBNZ's Statement in New Zealand time.

> Investors waiting for the US to turn before committing to income assets are waiting for a committee in which two members, at its last meeting, wanted to raise rates.

## 3. New Zealand's Two Open Questions: Jobs and a Government

**Jobs.** Stats NZ's September-quarter labour market statistics, released on 4 November, put the unemployment rate at **5.7%**, up from 5.6% in June (the highest since September 2015, on Westpac's reading), against economist expectations of a flat or lower outcome (The Post). Employment was 2,891,000 against 2,896,000 in June, the employment rate eased to 66.4%, and underutilisation held at 13.8%. Annual wage inflation, as measured by the labour cost index, was **2.1%** (2.0% in June). Westpac's reading was that the data were weaker than the RBNZ had expected, and wrote that "This data supports the views of those on the policy committee who were less convinced in October about the potential for second-round inflation pressures in the economy". ASB's acting chief economist Kim Mundy said there was "clearly still a lot of slack in the market" keeping a lid on wages. The RBNZ's own words a week earlier were that employment intentions "remain weak".

**A government.** New Zealand voted on 7 November. The Electoral Commission's preliminary count projects 131 seats: National 42, Labour 36, Greens 16, ACT 13, NZ First 12, the Opportunity Party 7 and Te Pāti Māori 5. National and its partners ACT and NZ First are on 67 seats; 66 are needed for a majority in an enlarged parliament. National and Labour each polled below 30% of the party vote, the lowest combined level since at least 1996 (Reuters). The preliminary figures are indicative: about 400,000 special votes are still to be counted, and the Commission expects to publish the official results on **Friday 27 November**.

Westpac's bulletin of 9 November is the most careful read of the arithmetic. It argues that National might lose perhaps two marginal electorates, and that special votes tend to reduce the centre-right bloc's share, so the bloc's 10-seat lead on election night could shrink by 1 to 4 seats, in which case it would need the Opportunity Party's support to govern. It also notes that a centre-right government would not change the RBNZ's mandate while a centre-left government likely would, and that serious coalition negotiations are unlikely to begin before 27 November. ANZ's view, as reported by NZ Adviser, is that neither major party's fiscal plan would materially change its economic or OCR outlook for now. We do not forecast the result. The point for an investor is the calendar: the official count and the RBNZ's Statement fall twelve days apart, so the Committee will set the OCR while coalition talks are still young.

> Two questions are still open: who governs, which the official count on 27 November will shape but may not settle, and what the Committee does on 9 December. Neither changes what a signed lease pays.

## 4. Singapore: A Quiet Month for MAS, a Firmer Base Rate

MAS does not set an interest rate. It sets a path for the Singapore dollar nominal effective exchange rate, the S$NEER, inside an undisclosed policy band, and adjusts the band's slope, width and centre at scheduled reviews. On 14 October it maintained the rate of appreciation of the policy band, with no change to the width or the centre, although every analyst in a Reuters poll had expected it to tighten. There has been no MAS decision since, and its next Monetary Policy Statement is due by **29 January 2027**.

The market has moved in the meantime. Three-month compounded SORA, the benchmark for SORA-linked Singapore property loans, was **1.3241%** on the 6 November publication, up from 1.2325% on 6 October, and 1.2568% on 23 October when we last reported it. That is a lift of about 9 basis points in a month, and it has risen every month since April, when it was 1.0182%. It is still less than half the RBNZ's OCR, and about 2.4 percentage points below the bottom of the Fed's target range, so local money has not become expensive. It is, however, moving in the same direction as borrowing costs in the other two economies we cover.

The data MAS and the Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry (METI) released in October still frame the picture. Core inflation was **2.0%** in September and headline **2.2%**. The Ministry of Energy, Trade and Industry's advance estimate put third-quarter GDP **5.4%** higher than a year earlier and **1.1%** higher than the previous quarter on a seasonally adjusted basis. URA reported on 23 October that Central Region office rents rose **0.8%** in the third quarter, prices rose **0.8%** and island-wide office vacancy eased to **10.0%** from 11.0%.

> Singapore tightens by letting its currency rise. Its borrowing costs have started to follow, slowly. On our reading, that still leaves freehold a store-of-value trade with cheap local debt, not an income story.

## 5. Translate Into Commercial Property: What a Pause Does to the Arithmetic

A pause is not a cut. The peak the banks expect, **3.25% to 4.00%**, is still above where the OCR is today, and the people who set mortgage and wholesale funding costs have not been told the date either. NZ Adviser reported on 9 November that two- to five-year fixed mortgage rates sit above 5% (Westpac), and that wholesale rates had fallen since the hold. For a wholesale investor the pause is a financing question: it changes the cost of leverage over the next six months far more than it changes the rent roll.

The income side of the arithmetic has not moved. JLL's September review has prime Auckland industrial trading at **5.00% to 5.50%** and Christchurch at **5.75% to 6.50%**. CBRE's Q3 2026 Asia Pacific cap rate survey (September figures) puts indicative cap rates for Singapore Grade A offices in core locations at **3.25% to 3.80%**, unchanged from March. Midpoint to midpoint, that is a gap of roughly **170 basis points** against Auckland and **260 basis points** against Christchurch. That gap is the reason the two markets are different trades. New Zealand pays for the risk in income. Singapore pays in tenure, scarcity and, for a foreign syndicate, the removal of a tax that rules out most of the residential market.

The RBNZ gave a reason to think the income side is supported rather than threatened. It said the economy "continues to recover", with export-exposed regions "benefiting from high commodity prices, strong demand for goods exports and increased tourist numbers", and that there are "some early signs of a recovery in residential investment, including in Auckland". It also said that domestic demand remains subdued outside the exporting centres, and that house prices continue to decline. For an industrial landlord, that is a supportive backdrop for tenants in logistics and export supply chains, and a less supportive one for anything that depends on household spending.

> A pause lengthens the list of things that can still go either way. A lease that has already been signed does not appear on it.

## 6. New Zealand Market Update

OCR **2.75%**, held on 28 October in a 4–3 vote; the next decision is the Monetary Policy Statement on **9 December**. Annual CPI inflation was **3.7%** in the September quarter, down from 4.1%, and the RBNZ expects **4.4%** in the December quarter on fuel prices. Unemployment is **5.7%**, wage inflation **2.1%**, and the Committee describes the recovery as continuing but uneven: strong for exporters and tourism-exposed regions, weak for households. ASB expects house prices to fall 1 to 2% over 2026, with inventory at its highest since March 2015 (NZ Adviser, 9 Nov).

New Zealand is still navigating a period of genuine difficulty, and we think it is more useful to say so than to frame it otherwise. For commercial property the practical implication is unchanged from July through October: **this is an income cycle, not a capital appreciation cycle**. The listings that work are the ones that pay through a hold, a hike and an unresolved election alike.

## 7. Singapore Market Update / The Freehold Case

In New Zealand the case is income. In Singapore the case is capital preservation, at a lower income. Both are valid. The mistake is applying the same framework to both.

Zoning still decides which Singapore assets an offshore syndicate can buy at all. IRAS charges Additional Buyer's Stamp Duty (ABSD) on residential property: 60% for foreigners and 65% for entities. For a whole building with land, IRAS classifies the property by its Master Plan zoning, so land zoned "Commercial" counts as non-residential and attracts ordinary Buyer's Stamp Duty only, and the Singapore Land Authority confirms that a foreign person can buy commercial and industrial property without approval under the Residential Property Act. That is why both Singapore buildings featured this month are commercial. One caution carries over from October: land zoned "Commercial & Residential" is treated as part residential, and ABSD applies to the residential share, so the zoning line on any shophouse is the first thing to confirm.

The yield is not the pitch. CBRE's own comparison, as reported by EdgeProp on 21 October, is that freehold and 999-year conservation shophouses in Districts 1 and 2 have transacted at between S$16 million and S$22 million, or S$5,000 to S$6,800 per square foot, over the last six months. That is the scale of capital preservation being offered: a small plot, a tight Master Plan, and a market that has not stopped trading.
